# Student build and power milestone

Build Your PC retains the OptiPlex-compatible parts picker and seven drag-to-seat steps. After placement, students choose a cable and match it to a named socket. Six routes appear in the service scene: board power, CPU power, fan, front switch, display and wall power. Routes and socket matching are illustrative; this is not a physical wiring guide. The M.2 SSD and illustrative slot-powered expansion card need no extra cable.

The power test rejects missing hardware, missing connections, an open drive cage or an unfitted side cover. Internal connection changes are blocked while wall power is connected; all socket changes are disabled during simulated operation. A successful test shows simulated POST, RAM, SSD and display checks. No operating system is booted, and actual electrical compatibility is not simulated. Cooling preparation adds a visible paste dot before cooler seating and four corner buttons in a simplified diagonal sequence (1, 3, 2, 4). Power-on requires both steps. Corner buttons represent fastening, not physical torque or a universal manufacturer sequence. Restart clears cooling preparation. The existing parts picker retains its supported-configuration checks.

Task Manager highlights process demand, explains resource release after ending a task, and collapses its challenge menu on phones. Completed challenges and XP, plus the PC Builder achievement, are stored in this browser's local storage. Storage failure falls back to session-only behavior. Assessment answers and unfinished assembly sessions are not saved. No student name or account is required.

The Latitude model adds mounting openings and power-stage detail, a smaller battery label, and projected labels for removed parts. Narrow stages show only the selected part's label. Existing guided service, cable gates, assessment, troubleshooting and Framework switching are retained.

Validation: 114 model and regression tests; TypeScript, production build and lint; browser interaction runs for desktop placement/wiring/power, Latitude learning flows, Task Manager and persisted XP. The deployed workflow repeats the desktop and Latitude checks.

Laptop fit pass: the CPU cooler contact now meets the die surface; heat pipes are flattened without shifting their route. SSD and Wi-Fi boards have keyed edges and mounting openings, with fixed board retainers retained. Wi-Fi retainer position is aligned to its new mounting opening. Speaker housings retain their footprint but use shallow oval diaphragms and grilles. RAM labels sit on the modules. Dimensions and internals remain service-reference estimates, not a manufacturer CAD model.

Detached laptop inspection: Tilt, Turn and Roll rotate the selected removable part around its own center once fully removed by global teardown or local explode. Rotation composes from the current teardown pose without accumulating transforms. Reset rotation preserves the lift; Reset part, selection changes, model changes and teardown changes clear inspection rotation. Seated components cannot rotate independently; the soldered CPU follows its motherboard. RAM, SSD and Wi-Fi have underside chip/pad detail. Controls operate in Explore mode and do not alter guided service steps.

Desktop board inspection pass: six fixed learning targets retain their own pickable geometry after batching: CPU socket, memory slots, expansion slots, M.2 storage socket, SATA ports and clock battery. Explore provides a touch-friendly list as an alternative to precise 3D clicking; each selection highlights the target and shows its name, job and connection. Direct picking respects occluding metal and installed components. The M.2 connector is aligned with the module contact edge, its support and screw remain on the motherboard, and the module has an open mounting hole. Small board details use varied circuit clusters instead of a uniform grid. This remains estimated teaching geometry, not measured CAD or a complete model of every electronic component.

Existing-activity completion pass: CPU/RAM/SSD/PCIe orientation is checked before dragging, with a correctly oriented translucent socket guide. The RAM PCB has a physical key notch and its contacts meet the slot at the revised seated height. RAM clips, SSD screw and expansion bracket visibly change when secured; all three are required by simulated power-on and cannot be changed with wall power connected. These steps do not simulate insertion force, torque or manufacturer-specific retention procedures.

Desktop main components now show a simple job, everyday example and connection. Rear/front USB, DisplayPort, HDMI, Ethernet, audio, PS/2, serial and power-button targets preserve individually pickable geometry. An alternate button list identifies the same port types. Completed builds allow inspection of visible installed hardware and cable routes; closed metal still blocks internal picking. Fine decorative circuit parts are not individually labelled.

Four desktop faults and six laptop follow-ups use test, evidence, matching fix and retest. Laptop case advancement requires verification, not just a component choice. Evidence and outcomes are scripted classroom cases and do not perform real hardware diagnostics. Similar symptoms can have other causes; examples do not certify a repair.

Task Manager records before/after readings at the same simulated moment when an app opens or ends. Light graphics in desktop and laptop lowers pixel density and disables shadows, preserving the activity state. Browser tests check phone-width overflow, port identification, light-mode controls, resource comparisons and all desktop diagnosis gates. This is not a performance benchmark on students' real low-spec hardware.
